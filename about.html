Who We Are

Pharaoh's Bites is a family
before it is a restaurant

In 1974 my grandmother Fatma sold her gold bangles to buy a second-hand oven and began selling feteer from a window on Abou El Feda Street. She had no menu. She had a book — forty pages of recipes in her own hand, some inherited from her mother in Assiut, some traded with neighbours over decades.

That book is still in the kitchen. It is stained, it is taped at the spine, and it is the final word on every dish we serve. When a supplier offers us a shortcut, we check the book. When a chef suggests an improvement, we check the book. It has not lost an argument yet.

Today the third generation runs the pass. The room is more beautiful than the window on Abou El Feda ever was. The food is exactly the same.

Heritage

Everything here has a source

Nothing at Pharaoh's Bites was invented in a test kitchen. Every dish came from a house, a village or a market stall, and someone in this family can tell you whose. What follows is how a bakery window became a kitchen — and why nothing about the food changed along the way.

Pharaoh's Bites timeline

1974

The window on Abou El Feda

Fatma Farid sells her gold bangles for a second-hand stone oven and starts selling feteer meshaltet through a window to the street. Two items. No sign above the door.

1981

The book is written down

Her son Farid insists the recipes are recorded before they are lost. Forty pages, in her hand, with weights measured in fistfuls and cups. It is still the reference today.

1989

Breakfast begins

Neighbours ask for foul with their bread. A single copper qidra goes on at midnight, and by morning there is a queue. The Egyptian breakfast table is born.

1996

The second oven

A dedicated bakery oven is built so bread and pastry never compete for the fire. Amira, sixteen, is given the four a.m. shift. She has kept it ever since.

2004

A kitchen, finally

The building next door is taken over and twenty-two covers are laid. The window stays open for the street — it still is, every morning at half past five.

2009

Hossam comes home

After six years in Lyon, the grandson returns to learn the book properly. He spends the first eleven months doing nothing but stretching dough.

2016

The Pharaoh's Table

A seven-course tasting menu traces the Egyptian day from breakfast to sweet. It sells out for eight months straight and has never come off the menu.

2021

The kitchen you order from now

The oven moved home and the doors closed to walk-ins. Everything now goes out boxed, hot, to whoever ordered it. The same forty pages.

Today

Third generation, first light

Eighteen thousand loaves a month, a hundred and twenty dishes, and an oven that has not been allowed to go cold in fifty-one years.

Handcrafted

Thirty folds, and not one of them optional

Feteer meshaltet is a test. The dough is rested, oiled, and then thrown — not rolled — until it is thin enough to read through. It is folded, oiled and folded again, thirty times, and the moment it is rushed you can taste it.

01

Rest

Four hours minimum. The gluten has to forget it was ever kneaded.

02

Throw

Stretched by hand in the air until light passes through. No pin, ever.

03

Fold

Clarified butter between every layer, thirty times, always the same direction.

04

Fire

Nine minutes on bare stone at four hundred degrees. It shatters, or it failed.

“If you are in a hurry, you should not be making feteer. And you should certainly not be eating it.”

Fatma Farid, 1974 — 2011

What We Stand On

Six promises, kept daily

None of these are marketing. Each one costs us money, time or sleep — which is precisely why they are worth writing down.

Fresh Ingredients

We buy from the same four families we bought from in the eighties. Vegetables arrive at dawn, herbs twice a day, and nothing is frozen — not once, not ever.

Traditional Recipes

Forty pages, three generations, zero substitutions. If a dish takes nine hours it takes nine hours, and we start it the night before.

Daily Handmade Baking

The oven is lit at four in the morning. Bread every twenty minutes, feteer stretched to order, pastry laminated by hand over three days.

Warm Hospitality

Nobody is rushed off a table at Pharaoh's Bites. Sit for four hours if you like. The tea is refilled without asking and the bread keeps arriving.

Authentic Flavours

We grind our own cumin and coriander weekly, press our own tahini, and cure our own basterma in fenugreek. The taste has a source.

Premium Quality

First-press olive oil, buffalo milk from the Delta, Aleppo pistachio, Siwa dates and salt. We would rather raise the price than lower the grade.
